GMKtec EVO-X5 Pro: Can a $6,399 Ryzen AI Max+ PRO 495 Mini PC Close the Gap With the Mac Studio and DGX Spark?

The GMKtec EVO-X5 Pro is not a budget machine. The 192GB model sells for $6,399. At that price it is a serious local-inference box, and the right question is whether it matches the Mac Studio M5 Ultra and Nvidia’s DGX Spark on the things that matter for running large models.

The short answer: it matches DGX Spark on memory bandwidth and beats it on capacity. It does not match the Mac Studio on bandwidth, and it trails both on software maturity.

What you are buying

The EVO-X5 Pro is built on AMD’s Ryzen AI Max+ PRO 495. It carries 192GB of LPDDR5X-8533 memory on a 256-bit interface. Tom’s Hardware reports that this configuration delivers up to about 273 GB/s of bandwidth. Eight 24GB chips are soldered close to the processor. The machine can allocate up to 160GB as VRAM for large models.

Storage is a 2TB or 4TB PCIe 4.0 SSD, with a $300 difference between them. There are three PCIe 4.0 M.2 slots. The mini PC also offers 10GbE, USB4 V2 and AMD DASH. Tom’s Hardware noted that GMKtec does not sell a bare-bones version, so buyers who already own a fast SSD cannot skip that cost.

The PRO 495 also has a higher-tier sibling in the same family. AMD said in May that the 495 would bring 192GB of memory and support for models of 300B parameters and up.

Memory is the story, and bandwidth is the limit

For local inference, two numbers decide most of the outcome. The first is capacity: how large a model fits. The second is bandwidth: how fast tokens come out. Generating each token means reading the active weights from memory, so bandwidth sets the ceiling on decode speed.

On bandwidth, the EVO-X5 Pro sits close to DGX Spark. Context Studios lists the DGX Spark, with its 128GB of unified memory, at 273 GB/s. The EVO-X5 Pro’s up to 273 GB/s is effectively the same figure. Context Studios lists standard Strix Halo at about 256 GB/s.

On capacity, the EVO-X5 Pro wins that comparison. 192GB beats 128GB, and it leaves room for models that the DGX Spark cannot hold.

The Mac Studio is in a different class on bandwidth. Context Studios lists the M5 Ultra at 1.2 TB/s, with unified memory of 96GB at the base configuration and 256GB or 512GB at the top. Apple says that bandwidth is 50 percent higher than the M3 Ultra’s 819 GB/s. The 512GB option arrives from late October, and only on the 80-core GPU configuration.

On paper, then, the Mac has roughly four times the bandwidth of the AMD box. That gap shows up in decode speed. In Context Studios’ table, the M5 Ultra manages 46 to 50 tokens per second on a 27B-class model, while the Strix Halo-class machines in the same table sit at 18 to 20 tokens per second. Context Studios attributes those Strix Halo figures to Ars Technica’s LM Studio tests. Those are different machines and different test setups, so treat them as directional. The direction is still clear.

Where it lands against DGX Spark

Both machines have about the same bandwidth. Decode speed on the Spark depends heavily on software. Context Studios measured 11.8 tokens per second on llama.cpp and 33 to 35 with SGLang plus multi-token prediction, the latter a community figure.

The Spark’s main advantage is its software stack. Context Studios names CUDA, clustering and concurrency as the Spark’s strengths. Tom’s Hardware’s review headline says the DGX Spark “beats out AMD’s Ryzen AI Max+ 395”. Tom’s also reported that the Spark’s review numbers show it outpacing the Ryzen parts on the workloads it tested.

The Spark’s price has moved. TechRadar, the outlet behind, lists it at about $5,000. Other listings have varied. AMD cited a $4,679 price in May, while a price comparison on Tom’s Hardware’s page shows $5,999.99. Prices on the Spark have risen across configurations, so check current pricing before comparing budgets.

On software, AMD’s Ryzen AI Halo platform, which uses the 395 chip, comes with full ROCm support and is optimized for LM Studio, ComfyUI and VS Code. That is useful. The Spark’s CUDA stack is the stronger case on software maturity.

AMD’s own comparisons favor its platform. It claimed a 7 percent edge on GPT-OSS 120B tokens per second and a 12 percent edge on Qwen 3.5 122B against the Spark. Those are vendor claims on AMD’s chosen test setup. I did not find independent benchmarks of the EVO-X5 Pro that confirm them.

The Microsoft RTX Spark Dev box

I found no reliable information on a Microsoft RTX Spark developer box. The sources cover Nvidia RTX Spark laptops, which start at $2,599 with pre-orders open. ASUS’s ProArt P16 with RTX Spark offers up to 128GB of unified memory and ships October 16. Dell’s XPS 16 Creator Edition with the same chip ships October 16 at up to 128GB, with pricing still unannounced. None of that is a desktop dev box, so I am not comparing it here.

Where the 495 stops making sense

The strongest case against the PRO 495 comes from a comparison with its sibling. The PRO 495 adds 64GB of memory over the 395 but only 6.6 percent more bandwidth. Extra capacity is only worth paying for if you need models that do not fit in 128GB. For smaller models, the bandwidth gain is too small to change decode speed much.

The same logic applies to the Mac Studio. Its bandwidth lead is the reason to consider it over the 495, provided your model fits in its memory configurations. The 495 makes the most sense when you need more than 128GB and cannot justify the Mac’s price or the Spark’s CUDA-first ecosystem.

Cost per token

AMD argues that local hardware pays for itself. Its model assumes a $3,999 Ryzen AI Halo, which opened for pre-orders in June. It also assumes $16.20 a month in electricity at a sustained 150W, and savings of up to $750 a month against cloud services. AMD’s break-even figure of six months depends on those assumptions. AMD’s own three-year estimate is a total of roughly $4,500 to $4,600, against more than $25K for cloud. Treat it as vendor math.

Context Studios ran its own numbers. By its estimate, local inference on the M5 Ultra costs roughly €1.70 to €2.70 per million output tokens, while the Qwen3.8-Flash API charges $0.47. If cost per token is the only metric you care about, the cloud still wins.

What to do about it

  • Start with your model size. If the models you need fit in 128GB, the EVO-X5 Pro’s extra memory buys you little. Compare the 395-class boxes and the DGX Spark first.
  • Decide whether you need CUDA. If your tooling depends on it, or you plan to cluster or serve many users, Context Studios recommends the DGX Spark. The similar bandwidth does not change that.
  • Check Mac Studio configurations. If your workload is mostly large mixture-of-experts models, the M5 Ultra’s bandwidth is hard to match at the sizes it supports. Wait for the 512GB configuration if you need that much memory. For dense models that fit into 32 GB, Context Studios says an RTX 5090 is still faster. Plan for the Mac’s power draw too: its power supply is rated at 480 W continuous.
  • Choose the SSD size deliberately. The 4TB option costs $300 more than the 2TB, and GMKtec does not sell a bare-bones version, so you cannot skip the drive.
  • Measure before you commit. I found no independent token-rate benchmarks of the EVO-X5 Pro. Run your own model on a return-eligible unit before buying at $6,399.
